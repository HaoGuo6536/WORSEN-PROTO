Two things you're right about, and one thing to reframe.
You're right that appearance is a first-run asset. A hunter's face is memorized by the third encounter, and no amount of art budget changes that. You're also right that character attachment won't happen in a roguelite with twenty-minute lives, so don't spend effort trying to build it. The reframe is this the hunters aren't the thing that has to be scary. The run is. A hunter is exactly as scary as what it costs you when it wins, and in a roguelite the thing at risk is never the character. It's the run state what you're carrying, how deep you are, what you'd have to redo.
Two different fears. The essay was about horror-fear, the feeling of rules being revoked. That feeling is novelty-dependent and decays, and you're correct that no game sustains it across a hundred runs. But there's a second fear that doesn't decay stake-fear, the feeling of having too much to lose right now. Poker's rules are fully known and poker is tense. Tarkov's enemies are memorized in an hour and every raid is frightening because you can lose everything you brought in. Lethal Company, Darkest Dungeon, same story. The monsters get learned; having something on the table never does. The games that stay scary are all stake-fear games seasoned with occasional horror-fear, not the reverse.
Dead by Daylight is the cautionary tale here, and it matters because your competitive-tag ambition pulls in its direction. DbD survivors have full knowledge of killer rules and still feel tension from hidden position and execution pressure, but for veterans it stopped being horror years ago and became a sport, because nothing personal is at risk. Stakes are what keep competitive tag on the horror side of that line.
What this means for WORSEN specifically
Keep the haul unbanked until you escape, and make the second sweep a wager. Every Golden Cake picked up during the collapse is a chip added to a pot you haven't won yet. That's push-your-luck, and it manufactures the one more fear on every run regardless of how well the player knows the hunters. If meta progression is fully protected, consider letting players bring something purchased into a run that dies with them. Stake needs teeth, and roguelites usually file them down.
The catch has to end the run, and it should be ugly. If getting caught costs little, nothing can be scary. The catch animation is where the in reality part lives it's what makes every near-miss mean something.
Hide state, not rules. Pac-Man shows all four ghosts at all times, and that, more than the graphics, is why it isn't scary. Let the player know everything about how hunters behave and starve them of where the hunters are right now. Sound as the primary channel, with occlusion and the occasional false positive; no markers; the look-back as the only hard confirmation. Position uncertainty is the one kind of unknown you can renew every single run.
On the 99% problem. You don't need the player scared 99% of the time; you need them at risk 99% of the time. And the rarity of behavioral subversion is correct rather than a flaw the model has to be trustworthy or betraying it is just noise. Frequent subversion feels unfair, not scary. So the subversion isn't what carries the game. Stakes carry it, and the subversion is a spike on top.
That said, there's a way to make rule-uncertainty ambient rather than a once-a-run event each run, one hunter rule is mutated, drawn from a pool the player learns over many runs, and the game tells you something is different but not what. The Thing applied to the hunters one of the four isn't behaving as you remember, and the first few minutes of every run are spent finding out which. It's fair because the pool is learnable and each mutation has a tell; it stays scary because the assignment is hidden.
The roguelite structure is also a novelty drip you're underusing. Endless escalation means you can introduce a new hunter type every N runs, or at depth thresholds, and every introduction is a return to real unknown for a few runs. That's the cadence at which horror-fear can be re-delivered.
Where to spend the art budget. Not the face. The things about an enemy that stay frightening after memorization are how it moves relative to you (Mr. X's footsteps, the alien in the vents), its sound signature, and what it does when it wins. Spend on gait, approach, audio, and the catch. Keep silhouettes readable at distance; you want type legibility, because the player's model is the thing the Director gets to betray.
The bar isn't horror every run. Nothing hits that. The bar is tense every run, horror sometimes, and the way you get there is by putting something on the table before the hunters ever show up.

What Dreadhalls actually does, stripped of the headset an oil lamp with limited fuel, so sight is a resource; a procedural maze with no map, so you're lost; no weapons and slow movement, so every encounter is a retreat; near-total silence with sparse spatial audio; and creatures whose rules revolve around whether you're looking at them, so the act of looking is itself the mechanic. Most of the fear comes from the last three, and those transfer.
The levers that transfer to a flat screen
Audio is the one presence channel you get almost in full. With headphones, HRTF binaural spatialization, proper occlusion, and enemies that announce themselves from behind and around corners, you recover a surprising fraction of what VR gives. Make audio the primary threat channel and tell players to wear headphones on the title screen. Then keep silence as the default no music bed, no ambient drone. Dreadhalls is quiet, and silence is what lets a footstep land.
Corners are your darkness. Dreadhalls limits information with the lamp; a Pac-Man maze limits it with geometry. Every corner is a hidden-state reveal, and a level built from corners produces the same I don't know what's around this pulse without the slowness that darkness imposes.
Reveals, not spawns. The moment in Dreadhalls that lands is seeing part of something before all of it eyes at the edge of lamplight, a shape emerging from a doorway. On a screen, have hunters enter view from occlusion, partially, and let the player's brain complete them.
Make looking committed. This is the biggest loss from VR, and the mouse is the culprit an instant 180 lets the player audit the room for free. Your look-back button is already the right idea, and you can push it a snap that shows a fixed frame and returns, rather than a scan. Keep forward turning responsive for the tag feel, but let the backward look be a glance you can't linger on.
Embodiment. Hands in frame, the player's own breathing and footsteps, a heartbeat that rises under pursuit and slightly masks the audio you're relying on. A body the player inhabits is the closest screen-substitute for presence, and it costs nothing in pace.
Spend startle deliberately. Nearly every scream in those videos is a startle event, and you can produce startles trivially on a screen. They decay and players resent them, so budget them one or two per run, earned by a broken expectation, plus the catch, which should be the loudest and ugliest moment in the game.
Two of Dreadhalls' levers you should refuse the slowness and the being-lost. Both fight the competitive-tag pace you've chosen, and the pace is a bigger design bet than any single scare.

Then you're aiming at something specific, and it's achievable, but only if the promise is stated precisely. The promise can't be every run scares everyone. Fixed content habituates; that's not a design problem, it's a nervous system problem, and no amount of craft beats it. The promise that is achievable is there is always a rung above you that scares you, and you will climb to it. That's what Make it Worse actually means as a mechanism. The only fear that survives repetition is fear the player ratchets up themselves, and a thrill-seeker's entire psychology is that they seek the edge. Your job isn't to keep one experience scary forever. It's to make sure the ladder never runs out of rungs above wherever a given player currently stands.
Aim at anxiety, spike with terror. These are different emotions with different habituation curves. Startle-terror habituates within hours. Anxiety, the anticipatory kind, barely habituates as long as its ingredients stay real uncertainty about the outcome, something to lose, a clock, and no way to resolve the uncertainty without acting. Ranked-match anxiety in competitive games is proof that this survives thousands of hours; people with 3,000 hours in a game still get shaky hands in a decisive round. So the base state of a WORSEN run should be sustained anxiety, and terror is the punctuation.
Design the freeze directly. Can't push forward is a specific, producible state. It happens when the player faces a threshold with a known threat beyond it, a high cost of being wrong, and no safe alternative waiting is also bad, because the collapse clock is running and hunters converge. Build these deliberately doorway, hunter audible beyond it, cake counter says you have to go through, timer says you can't wait. The freeze is what two bad options feel like. And give it an honorable exit. A bail with a penalty (extract early, lose most of the haul) converts I can't from a rage-quit into a decision the player made and will want to beat next time. Thrill seekers quitting the app is a loss; thrill seekers choosing to bank and run, then coming back at 1am to try the door again, is the loop.
What worse must mean. This is the part that determines whether the slogan works. Difficulty and fear are different axes, and most games' hard modes climb the wrong one. More hunter HP, faster hunters, more damage those produce frustration, and frustration displaces fear. The ladder has to climb fear axes
Information remove the look-back, remove hunter audio cues, remove the cake counter, dim the level.
Unpredictability more rule mutations per run, mutations without tells, hunter count hidden and variable.
Stakes wager banked Golden Cakes on the run, bring-in items lost on death, tiers where meta progress is at risk.
Agency fewer tools, shorter stamina, no bail option.
Time faster collapse, a clock on the first sweep.
Notice these are all the essay's thesis turned into a menu. Every worsening removes something the player relies on, and the game should say so explicitly You are giving up the look-back. The player choosing to revoke their own safety rules is the purest version of what we started with, and it's self-renewing because the player picks the rule they've come to lean on most.
Stakes must climb faster than lethality. A rung where the player dies constantly is a rung where death stops mattering. The top of the ladder should be survivable by a skilled player and catastrophic to lose, not unwinnable. Push the wager, not the kill rate. The most frightened player in your game should be the one with their whole bank on the table at a rung they can just handle.
Let the hell persist. Players build their own hell suggests worsenings that don't reset each run. Curses that stay until you climb out of them, a debt that compounds when you die, a run that starts where the last one got worse. That gives the ladder memory and makes the player the author of their own situation, which is the framing that keeps it from feeling unfair you did this.
Outpacing habituation over the long tail. Even the ladder has a top, so feed it. New hunter types unlock at depth, so the top rungs contain things the player has never seen. The mutation pool grows with runs, so the rule-uncertainty keeps refreshing. And in-run worsening, an optional make it worse verb for extra reward mid-run, lets the player escalate inside the experience, not just at the menu. Darkest Dungeon's torch is the best precedent dimming it is scarier and pays better, and players do it to themselves.
What you'll get from this is not a game that's frightening to everyone every time. It's a game where a veteran at rung 5 is calm and bored, chooses rung 30 because that's where they feel something, and at rung 30, with everything wagered and the look-back gone, cannot make themselves open the door. That player is exactly as scared as the first-timer was, for completely different reasons, and it'll still be true on run 500.
